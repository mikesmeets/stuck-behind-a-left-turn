The worries

The same worries come up every time

Every town that has done this heard the same things first: traffic will back up for miles, the shops will die, the fire trucks won't get through, drivers will race down our side streets. These worries are common, and they are reasonable. Time and time again, they have also proved to be unfounded. Here is what was said before, and what was found after.

“Traffic will back up for miles”

Before · Rye, New York

Mamaroneck warned that Rye’s road diet would back traffic up for three miles.

Recounted by Brian Dempsey, chair of Rye’s Traffic and Pedestrian Safety Committee

After · Rye, New York

The diet “has been a huge success.” Crashes on the stretch fell 67%, and at the Osborn Road intersection by the elementary school, by more than 90%.

Doug French, former mayor of Rye

Before · Milton, Massachusetts

Route 28 was a four-lane road through neighbourhoods with a crash history, and residents warned that taking a lane would jam the intersections and push commuters onto the side streets.

Raised through the autumn 2025 pilot, at listening sessions and Select Board meetings

After · Milton, Massachusetts

“I live on that road. It’s dramatically better… It’s very successful, and I’ve heard from a lot of people that they love it… You’ve got to deal with traffic, I understand that. It’s just a safer road.”

John Driscoll, Milton Select Board, May 2026. MassDOT kept the road diet: injury crashes down, speeds down 6 to 8 mph

Before · Lawyers Road, Reston, Virginia

At the community meetings before VDOT restriped the road, residents raised “concerns regarding potential traffic congestion” and doubted the bike lanes were needed.

FHWA’s account of the 2009 public meetings

After · Lawyers Road, Reston, Virginia

VDOT went back and asked the neighbours. 69% said travel times had not increased — even though 59% said speeds had dropped. 69% said the road seemed safer, and 74% agreed the project had improved it. Crashes fell 70%.

VDOT community survey, autumn 2010, and its five-year safety study

Before · Barton Springs Road, Austin

Those opposing the project “raised concerns about potential traffic congestion. They fear that reducing lanes could result in gridlock on a crucial artery.”

KUT, as Austin began the 12-month pilot, July 2023

After · Barton Springs Road, Austin

“A much safer street, lower risk for everybody involved, including drivers and people outside of vehicles. And [we were] able to do that with minimal operational impacts.” Drivers going over 40 mph fell 65%, about 500 fewer a day.

Lewis Leff, assistant director, Austin Transportation and Public Works, May 2024

Before · Prospect Park West, Brooklyn

Opponents were sure enough that taking a lane would jam the street that they took the city’s transportation department to court to have the redesign ripped out.

A group of local residents sued NYCDOT in 2011

After · Prospect Park West, Brooklyn

“Peak traffic volumes and travel times remain stable after implementation.” Crashes fell 16% and injury crashes fell 63%. Drivers breaking the speed limit went from 74% to 20%. The lawsuit was dismissed.

NYCDOT’s before-and-after monitoring

Before · Merrimon Avenue, Asheville

A petition signed by nearly 900 people said the conversion was “creating backups, delays, safety issues, and driving more traffic onto residential streets.” A shop owner on the avenue called the drive “horrible, it’s literally horrible.”

“Save Merrimon Ave Before It’s Too Late,” and Frank Dosier of the Musician’s Workshop, November 2022

After · Merrimon Avenue, Asheville

NCDOT kept it. Its monitoring found total crashes down 24%, injury crashes down 6%, vehicle speeds down, and more people riding bikes — most of them now in the road rather than on the sidewalk.

NCDOT safety and mobility summaries, June 2024 and August 2026

See it in the traffic simulation

“It will kill the shops”

Before · Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines, Iowa

“Business owners were evenly split between those who thought the changes would hurt their business and those that thought business would increase.”

Surveyed before the 2010 trial

After · Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines, Iowa

A majority of business owners “reported they saw no effect,” and 23% reported an increase in shopping along the corridor. The trial was made permanent.

Surveyed after the trial

Before · East Boulevard, Charlotte, North Carolina

A commuter route carrying 16,000 to 21,400 vehicles a day through a historic district of shops, cafes and homes, with 85th-percentile speeds of 43 mph on a 35-mph street and some drivers doing 50 to 70. The neighbourhood plan asked for a “main street.”

Charlotte ran traffic simulations first, then converted the first stretch from four lanes to three in 2006

After · East Boulevard, Charlotte, North Carolina

“Travel times in the corridor remained constant” and “outdoor dining significantly increased along the corridor after project implementation.” Asked afterwards whether to carry the four-to-three conversion through the last stretch, 77% of those surveyed said yes.

Project for Public Spaces, on the first two phases. Respondents “touted the increased safety of the road for drivers, bicyclists, and pedestrians.” Phase III was built in 2011

Before · Hillsborough Street, Raleigh

Two hundred businesses lined the street, and even the people leading the project expected them to suffer: “It will be uncomfortable while it’s going on, even painful. We’ll work hard to see the businesses that start out here stay here.”

George Chapman, president of the Hillsborough Street Partnership, as construction began in 2009. The idea, he said, had been “not easily accepted in the general community”

After · Hillsborough Street, Raleigh

Four lanes became two, with roundabouts, on-street parking and wider sidewalks. Vehicle crashes fell 23%, and new private investment along the corridor rose from $150 million to $200 million.

Project for Public Spaces’ case study. Sidewalk mileage along the corridor has nearly doubled since 2013

Before · Route 62, Village of Hamburg, New York

Worried about future congestion, NYSDOT proposed rebuilding the village’s main street to carry more traffic: wider lanes, a third lane, and much of the on-street parking gone. A lifelong resident asked, “What are you doing with the trees and the people?” She said state officials told her: “We have to get the traffic through.”

Susan Burns, quoted in The New York Times, on NYSDOT’s original plan in the early 2000s

After · Route 62, Village of Hamburg, New York

“Our entire village is transformed. Not a day goes by in my store I don’t hear about how everyone loves our village. This project fulfilled every expectation and then some.”

Laura Hackathorn, village trustee. The village voted 4–1 for traffic calming over widening, and NYSDOT built it: crashes down 57%, serious injuries down 90%, speeds from 30 to 19 mph, and the street still carries 500 to 600 heavy trucks a day. Commercial building permits in the village rose from 16 in 2005 to 96 in 2010, and property values along the corridor more than doubled. NYSDOT’s own verdict: the project “has the potential to have far-reaching and positive effects on the quality of life for village residents”

Before · Edgewater Drive, Orlando

The fear was gridlock and diversion, and at first it looked founded. When the lane came out, weekday traffic fell from 20,500 vehicles a day to 18,100 — drivers “taking another route out of fear of increased congestion.” Some called for the street to be put back to four lanes.

FHWA on Orlando’s before-and-after study, and the city’s own public process

After · Edgewater Drive, Orlando

The corridor gained 77 new businesses and 560 jobs. Property values rose 80% alongside the street and 70% within half a mile. Collisions fell 40% and injuries 71%; traffic dipped 12% and then returned to its old level.

When Edgewater Drive was resurfaced again in 2012, stakeholders “made no requests to restore Edgewater to a four-lane road”

Sometimes the shops ask for it. In Maplewood, New Jersey the push to narrow Springfield Avenue came from the township’s Economic Development Commission, as part of a strategy to revive the business district and raise property values. The aim was to slow the traffic and make the avenue a “handsome and walkable ‘boulevard.’”

What the studies found about local business

“Fire trucks and ambulances won't get through”

This is the worry that stops projects, and the one with the clearest answer: the center turn lane gives responders a lane nobody else is in.

Before · Woodbury, New Jersey

Woodbury Police were “initially opposed, citing concerns over potentially slower emergency response times.”

FHWA’s account of the proposal for South Broad Street

After · Woodbury, New Jersey

“The Road Diet did not affect emergency vehicle response times. Responders feel it is now easier and safer to reach their destination.”

Reed Merinuk, retired police chief, Woodbury PD

Before · Ocean City, New Jersey

There was “skepticism from emergency responders and the public” before the change on West Avenue, the main route for emergency vehicles.

FHWA’s account

After · Ocean City, New Jersey

The new configuration is “leaps and bounds safer.”

Brian Hopely, police traffic sergeant, Ocean City Police

Before · Main Street, Asbury Park, New Jersey

The city opposed NJDOT’s plan for its own main street, citing summer tourist traffic, buses and loading zones, and “how emergency responders would traverse along the narrowed roadway.”

The Asbury Park Sun, on the city’s objection to the state’s road diet, 2017

After · Main Street, Asbury Park, New Jersey

“It all goes back to public safety. The City wouldn’t commit to this plan until our police department, our fire department, DPW, OEM, and Planning were all on board. It is worth trying and this plan will not affect public safety.”

Mayor John Moor, once the city had taken its emergency departments’ concerns to NJDOT, asked for more traffic counts, and had the design changed. NJDOT has since built the road diet

More on emergency response

“Drivers will cut through our side streets”

Before · What the worry sounds like

“Increasing traffic on neighborhood streets always comes up as a worry.”

Peter Lagerwey, former coordinator of Seattle’s pedestrian and bicycle program, in FHWA’s Public Roads

After · What the counts show

“But it’s just something that doesn’t happen with road diets. People stay on the street.”

The same paragraph. Seattle monitors the neighbourhood streets before and after every conversion

Before · West Hartford, Connecticut

Neighbors north of Fern Street feared that drivers pushed off North Main Street would race through the residential streets instead.

Raised during the trial, and made a formal test of it

After · West Hartford, Connecticut

“The level of diversion to other roadways has not been significant.” The town counted, and the trial was made permanent that month: “Based on the results … the trial has achieved all of our objectives.”

Greg Sommer, town engineer, and acting town manager Rick Ledwith, June 2022. The objectives were fewer crashes, lower speeds and better access for people walking and biking

Before · Stone Way, Seattle

“We need the road. We need to move traffic, particularly trucks. It’s not a good idea to narrow the street down because traffic will overflow on other streets. This is our arterial. We want the four lanes of traffic.”

Suzie Burke, a Fremont business owner with property on Stone Way, in The Seattle Times, October 12, 2006

After · Stone Way, Seattle

“Motor vehicle traffic has not diverted to neighborhood streets. Daily traffic counts on parallel streets are down by 12 to 34 percent.” Peak-hour volume fell about 5%, so capacity held; top-end speeding fell more than 80% and pedestrian collisions fell 80%.

SDOT’s before-and-after study, 2010. FHWA files the case under “despite early opposition, Road Diet produces great results”

Sometimes the critics say it themselves

Des Moines put a two-mile road diet on Ingersoll Avenue in 2010 as a six-month trial, on a street carrying 11,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day, and promised to put the four lanes back if the objections proved right. Crashes fell by half, traffic volumes did not drop, and the city council voted to keep it. The local paper had opposed it.

Before · Des Moines, Iowa

“We were critical of the Des Moines City Council for its decision to approve the restriping of Ingersoll Avenue to change it from four lanes to three lanes…”

Editorial board, Cityview, Central Iowa’s independent weekly

After · Des Moines, Iowa

“…On all accounts, we were wrong. Our concerns proved to be unwarranted.”

The same editorial board, “Road diet makes Ingersoll fit,” November 18, 2010

Before · North Main Street, West Hartford

A former client told the lawyer Scott Franklin, a few years earlier, that the idea of a road diet on North Main Street was “the dumbest thing ever.”

Recounted by Franklin, a past chair of the town’s Pedestrian and Bicycle Commission

After · North Main Street, West Hartford

He recently heard from that former client, who apologized. “It’s a great testament that our town has bought into this. It’s real change, and it’s a change definitely for the positive.”

At the public information session on the trial, April 2022. A resident of the street put it another way: “I no longer feel like I’m going to die when I’m walking or mowing my lawn”
