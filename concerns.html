The worries

The same worries come up every time

Every town that has done this heard the same things first: the fire trucks won't get through, traffic will back up for miles, drivers will race down our side streets, the shops will die. Those worries are reasonable. They are also testable, and other towns have tested them. Here is what was said before, and what was found after.

“Fire trucks and ambulances won't get through”

This is the worry that stops projects, and the one with the clearest answer: the center turn lane gives responders a lane nobody else is in.

Before · Woodbury, New Jersey

Woodbury Police were “initially opposed, citing concerns over potentially slower emergency response times.”

FHWA’s account of the proposal for South Broad Street

After · Woodbury, New Jersey

“The Road Diet did not affect emergency vehicle response times. Responders feel it is now easier and safer to reach their destination.”

Reed Merinuk, retired police chief, Woodbury PD

Before · Ocean City, New Jersey

There was “skepticism from emergency responders and the public” before the change on West Avenue, the main route for emergency vehicles.

FHWA’s account

After · Ocean City, New Jersey

The new configuration is “leaps and bounds safer.”

Brian Hopely, police traffic sergeant, Ocean City Police

Before · Asheville, North Carolina

“Yes, I was surprised. To me, you only have this one main artery here.”

Jerry Vehaun, Buncombe County emergency services director, on the Charlotte Street proposal, March 2019

After · Asheville, North Carolina

“Improving transportation safety for all users is a top priority for the city, and the Charlotte Street Road Diet has successfully achieved this by reducing speeds and crashes.”

Jessica Morriss, assistant transportation director, City of Asheville, March 2022. Left-turn crashes fell 93% and sideswipes 50%

Then somebody measured it. University of Iowa researchers compared 3,872 fire department trips in Cedar Rapids on streets before and after four lanes became three. Response times “did not meaningfully differ.” In West Hartford, Connecticut, police, fire and ambulance services were a formal part of the trial evaluation; they raised no life-safety concerns and supported making it permanent. Asheville’s own follow-up is the honest gap in this set: it measured crashes, speeds and volumes, but never went back and measured response times.

More on emergency response

“Traffic will back up for miles”

Before · Rye, New York

Mamaroneck warned that Rye’s road diet would back traffic up for three miles.

Recounted by Brian Dempsey, chair of Rye’s Traffic and Pedestrian Safety Committee

After · Rye, New York

The diet “has been a huge success.” Crashes on the stretch fell 67%, and at the Osborn Road intersection by the elementary school, by more than 90%.

Doug French, former mayor of Rye

Before · Nickerson Street, Seattle

“One concern that arose during project planning was that vehicles would be diverted to alternate routes in an attempt to bypass traffic on Nickerson Street.”

SDOT’s own record of the objection

After · Nickerson Street, Seattle

The street carried 18,563 vehicles a weekday before and 18,364 after — more traffic than Boston Post Road, on one lane each way. Drivers going 10+ mph over the limit fell from 17% to 1.4% one way and 38% to 1.5% the other.

SDOT before-and-after report. Traffic on the two streets named as likely bypasses fell as well

Then somebody measured it. Roanoke, Virginia studied Elm Avenue before and after using INRIX travel-time data and found travel times fell 5% to 10%, which city staff put down partly to better signal coordination — an improvement that happened despite the loss of a lane. Maplewood, New Jersey expected the same pattern: intersection level of service improving with retimed signals, while end-to-end service along the avenue slipped slightly.

And where it didn't hold. On Maryland Avenue in Wilmington, Delaware, morning travel times fell about 3.5% in both directions, but evening travel times rose: about 5% eastbound and 24% westbound, roughly two minutes over a 3.3-mile run. DelDOT's consultant said plainly that the longer evening times “were anticipated due to the reduction in roadway capacity.”

See it in the traffic simulation

“Drivers will cut through our side streets”

Before · West Hartford, Connecticut

Neighbors north of Fern Street feared that drivers pushed off North Main Street would race through the residential streets instead.

Raised during the trial, and made a formal test of it

After · West Hartford, Connecticut

The town counted traffic on those streets during the trial. The cut-through didn’t happen, and the trial was made permanent in June 2022.

VHB’s final report for the Town of West Hartford

Before · Stone Way, Seattle

“We need the road. We need to move traffic, particularly trucks. It’s not a good idea to narrow the street down because traffic will overflow on other streets. This is our arterial. We want the four lanes of traffic.”

Local business owners, quoted in The Seattle Times, October 2006

After · Stone Way, Seattle

“Motor vehicle traffic has not diverted to neighborhood streets. Daily traffic counts on parallel streets are down by 12 to 34 percent.” Peak-hour capacity held, top-end speeding fell more than 80%, and pedestrian collisions fell 80%.

SDOT’s before-and-after study, 2010. FHWA files the case under “despite early opposition, Road Diet produces great results”

The honest footnote. Traffic did move in West Hartford, but onto another arterial rather than onto side streets: evening-peak volumes fell 22% on North Main Street and rose 16% on parallel Trout Brook Drive, partly the pandemic. About a third of the 1,914 people surveyed did not back making the trial permanent. In Asheville, where residents raised the same worry in 2019, the city found traffic volumes and speeds on the side streets they named, Madison Avenue and Washington Street, had gone down.

“It will kill the shops”

Before · Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines, Iowa

“Business owners were evenly split between those who thought the changes would hurt their business and those that thought business would increase.”

Surveyed before the 2010 trial

After · Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines, Iowa

A majority of business owners “reported they saw no effect,” and 23% reported an increase in shopping along the corridor. The trial was made permanent.

Surveyed after the trial

Before · Valencia Street, San Francisco

“Before the Valencia Street project was implemented, the community was split between excitement and fear of what might result.”

Michael Sallaberry, transportation engineer, San Francisco Municipal Transportation Agency

After · Valencia Street, San Francisco

“After it was implemented, it was embraced by nearly everyone as a success — not only by people who ride bikes, but those who walk, businesses along the street and the neighborhood in general.”

The same engineer. The merchants’ association said it “has made the road seem like more of a destination than an arterial”

Sometimes the shops ask for it. In Maplewood, New Jersey the push to narrow Springfield Avenue came from the township’s Economic Development Commission, as part of a strategy to revive the business district and raise property values. The aim was to slow the traffic and make the avenue a “handsome and walkable ‘boulevard.’” San Francisco ran Valencia Street as a one-year trial with baseline data collected first, then took the results to public hearings; had the board of supervisors rejected it, the street would have gone back to four lanes.

What the studies found about local business

Sometimes the critics say it themselves

Des Moines put a two-mile road diet on Ingersoll Avenue in 2010 as a six-month trial, on a street carrying 11,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day, and promised to put the four lanes back if the objections proved right. Crashes fell by half, traffic volumes did not drop, and the city council voted to keep it. The local paper had opposed it.

Before · Des Moines, Iowa

“We were critical of the Des Moines City Council for its decision to approve the restriping of Ingersoll Avenue to change it from four lanes to three lanes…”

Editorial board, Cityview, Central Iowa’s independent weekly

After · Des Moines, Iowa

“…On all accounts, we were wrong. Our concerns proved to be unwarranted.”

The same editorial board, “Road diet makes Ingersoll fit,” November 18, 2010

“Why should we be the guinea pig?”

You wouldn’t be. Virginia’s transportation research council inventoried 66 road diets built in that state alone since 2010, about 39 miles of road, and surveyed the towns and cities that built them. Of the projects that drew public feedback, half was positive and half was mixed. Not one locality reported that the feedback was mostly negative.

The examples, one by one

What to ask for here

The pattern in the places where this went well is not that the worries were dismissed. It is that they were written down and tested. Woodbury’s police were brought in and the design changed. West Hartford counted the side streets its neighbors were worried about. In Maplewood, NJDOT put cones down first and ran the numbers before making anything permanent. Des Moines ran a trial and surveyed the businesses on both sides of it.

Those are reasonable things to ask of NYSDOT and of our own fire and police departments, and they are worth raising at the next public meeting.
