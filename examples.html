Examples

Road diets on streets like ours

A road diet isn't an experiment. State transportation departments have built them on busy main streets for decades, including on state highways that carry more traffic than Boston Post Road.

Woodbury is one of the towns in NJDOT's video on its road diets. See Broad Street on Google Street View.

The closest comparison

Route 45, Woodbury, New Jersey

Broad Street in Woodbury is a state highway through a county seat: a historic downtown, strip centers and blocks of homes, much like Boston Post Road through Larchmont and Mamaroneck. In 2012 NJDOT converted 0.8 miles of it, from Carpenter Street to Red Bank Avenue, from four lanes to one lane each way with a center turn lane and bike lanes, keeping parking where it could.

18,449vehicles a day on Route 45 in Woodbury, 2012
14,958on Boston Post Road in Mamaroneck, 2024; 10,104 in Larchmont

NJDOT made the change to address pedestrian safety and congestion at intersections with no room for left turns. Woodbury's police opposed it at first, worried about response times. After it was built, the retired police chief said it did not affect them.

Nearby examples

US Route 9, Town of Ossining and Briarcliff Manor · 2007

A state highway past strip commercial

South of the Village of Ossining, Route 9 runs past strip shopping and a string of side streets and driveways with no traffic signals. It was converted from four lanes to one lane each way with a center turn lane in 2007, when it carried 15,308 vehicles a day, about what the Post Road carries in Mamaroneck today. No bike lanes were added.

In 2021 a study for the Village of Ossining found that extending the road diet north through the village was feasible.

Commerce Drive, Fairfield, Connecticut

Strip commercial, at Rye Neck's traffic

A strip of shopping centers and restaurants carrying about 10,500 vehicles a day, close to Boston Post Road between Rye Neck and the Rye border (9,740 in 2025). It was converted to one lane each way with a center turn lane.

US Route 1, Fairfield, Connecticut · 2025

The same road, explained in public

Connecticut restriped its own Boston Post Road, Route 1 from Pease Avenue to Beaumont Street, from four lanes to three with a center turn lane in 2025, with resurfacing, signal upgrades and curb ramps. It carries about 14,500 vehicles a day, close to the Post Road in Mamaroneck.

What stands out is how CTDOT explained it. Its project team held a public meeting in Fairfield, in person and streamed on Webex, YouTube and local TV, and walked through why the road needed a change, what a road diet is, what it would do to traffic and the crash record behind it, then took questions. It posted the recording and project materials online and gave residents two weeks to send comments, inviting them “to share their feedback with the CTDOT project team to incorporate into the design.”

Boston Post Road (US 1), Rye, New York

Our own road, one town over

Boston Post Road already has a road diet in Rye, on a stretch near Milton and Greenhaven. It's a less direct comparison than Woodbury or Fairfield, because that stretch is all homes and no shops. What it shows is how a change that looks ordinary today can be contested when it's proposed.

Livingston Avenue, New Brunswick, New Jersey · 2020

Studied first, then built

Livingston Avenue is a wide county road leading into downtown New Brunswick. Before changing it, the city had Rutgers' Voorhees Transportation Center study the idea. The researchers modeled the traffic and found the added delay would be small, with no drop to an unacceptable level of service, and estimated about 19% fewer crashes. The safety benefits outweighed the extra travel time.

The city and Middlesex County converted it in 2020 to one lane each way with a center turn lane, bike lanes and pedestrian improvements.

The 19% is the study's projection from other road diets, and we haven't found a before-and-after study of Livingston Avenue itself. A 2016 critique in the same journal argued the study's method overstated the benefits; the authors published a rejoinder.

North Main Street, West Hartford, Connecticut · 2021–22

Winning over a skeptical town with a trial

A 1.5-mile residential arterial carrying 12,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day. The idea took seven years: a regional study in 2015, a trial budgeted in 2019, and a trial striped in August 2021, one lane each way with a center turn lane. Instead of arguing about the worries, the town measured each one during the trial.

  • Cut-through traffic. Neighbors north of Fern Street feared drivers would race through side streets. Counts showed they didn't.
  • Crashes. 16 on North Main Street in the seven trial months, against 18 to 51 in the same months of each of the four years before.
  • Emergency response. Police, fire and ambulance raised no life-safety concerns and supported the change.
  • The public. Of 1,914 people surveyed, two-thirds wanted it made permanent. Complaints centered on signal timing and drivers learning the center lane, and the town adjusted both.

The town made it permanent in June 2022.

Some traffic did move: evening-peak volumes fell 22% on North Main Street and rose 16% on parallel Trout Brook Drive, partly the pandemic. About a third of survey respondents didn't back making it permanent.

Bigger studies

Virginia · Virginia Transportation Research Council, 2020 and 2022

One state's road diets, reviewed and then measured

The 2020 review

Virginia's DOT asked its research council to take stock of every road diet built in the state since 2010. It found 66 projects covering about 39 miles and asked the cities and counties that built them how they turned out.

Did the road diet meet its goals? Local officials on 25 projects:

  • 18 met their goals
  • 3 mixed results
  • 3 too new to judge
  • 1 unknown
  • None drew mostly negative feedback from the public; of the 18 that drew any, half was mostly positive and half mixed.
  • No general congestion problems, the localities reported.
  • No practically significant change in average speeds on the Fairfax County road diets the researchers measured.

Mixed: three Richmond projects where bike lanes ended awkwardly or a two-way bike track added delay. These are the judgments of the officials who built the projects, not measurements, and crashes were outside the study's scope.

The 2022 follow-up: crashes

Researchers at the same council then compared crashes before and after at 36 stretches of road and 39 intersections where Virginia turned four lanes into three between 2009 and 2018. They used the empirical Bayes method, which corrects for changes in traffic and for sites picked right after a bad year. Every result was statistically significant.

−64%

crashes that killed or injured someone, along the converted stretches. Total crashes fell 38%.

−46%

injury and fatal crashes at intersections on the converted roads. Total crashes there fell 35%.

2,600–17,000

vehicles a day on the roads studied. Boston Post Road carries 10,000 to 15,000.

And Virginia kept building them

We count at least 10 more completed since 2019, from Seminary Road in Alexandria and Wilson Boulevard in Arlington to VDOT's own four-to-three conversions of Route 360 on the Northern Neck. Since 2019 Virginia's highway safety program has also paid for road diets on local roads as a proven, low-cost safety fix, often restriped during repaving, the way NYSDOT plans to do it here. That count is a floor: it includes only projects we could confirm were finished.

Delaware · DelDOT before-and-after studies, 2017–2024

Traffic held; the crashes left turns cause fell

Delaware's DOT publishes before-and-after studies of its road diets. Three of them look like Boston Post Road: four lanes turned into one lane each way with a center turn lane, on busy arterials.

+10%

volume on Philadelphia Pike in Claymont in the years after its road diet, while the parallel road, Governor Printz Boulevard, lost 36%. Drivers moved onto the road diet, not off it, though the report says regional shifts may play a part.

−13%

total crashes on Philadelphia Pike, three years before against three after. Angle crashes fell 26%.

16 → 5

angle crashes a year on Cleveland Avenue. On Maryland Avenue in Wilmington, 28 a year fell to 15 in the first year.

Not every number moved the right way. Cleveland Avenue's own summary says total crashes did not fall significantly. Rear-end and sideswipe crashes rose on some corridors. On Philadelphia Pike, crashes involving people walking or biking went from 1 to 6, all at the ends where the road goes back to four lanes. Maryland Avenue has one year of after data, and none of the studies tested statistical significance.
