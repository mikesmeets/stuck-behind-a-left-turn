Process

Requested locally, paid for by the state

Our municipalities asked for this project. NYSDOT designed it, and state and federal money pays for it. The design isn't final, and there is still time to weigh in.

Where it came from

When NYSDOT planned to repave Boston Post Road, it noticed that downtown Larchmont, from Beach to Alden, has an unusually high number of crashes for its traffic. So it added a Complete Streets safety component to the repaving.

Public outreach

NYSDOT lists the project as "In Development." It has shown alternatives at two public open houses and keeps refining the design based on engineering and public input.

  1. 2024Larchmont, the Village of Mamaroneck and the Town of Mamaroneck each pass a resolution asking NYSDOT to extend Complete Streets the full length of Route 1. A big reason was the ten schools along the route, with more than 6,000 students.
    Village of Larchmont · March 4, 2024 Village of Mamaroneck · January 8, 2024 Town of Mamaroneck · January 2024
  2. July 2024First stakeholder meeting with the municipalities and the schools, on July 31.
  3. November 2024Second stakeholder meeting. A road diet is on the table from the start: NYSDOT’s own slides list it among the corridor improvements, and set out the lane widths a three-lane design would use.
    The meeting, November 22, 2024 “Multi-Modal Improvements – Bike, Transit, Road Diet” One of the three-lane configurations
  4. June 2025First public open house. NYSDOT’s boards showed four conceptual cross-sections for the corridor, three of them with bike lanes and a two-way left-turn lane. People could talk to the project team face to face and write directly on roll plots of their own streets.

    NYSDOT counted it all up and reported back at the next open house: 89 comment sheets, 26 emails, 49 post-its on the boards and 358 on the roll plots. Safety came first, ahead of infrastructure, bike lanes and everything else.

  5. April 2026Second open house, and the plan is revealed: roll plots drawn over aerial photographs, showing the proposed design block by block along the whole corridor. Residents could walk the length of their own street on the table and give the project team direct feedback on what NYSDOT was proposing.

    The four roll plots cover the corridor end to end: Roll plot 1 · Roll plot 2 · Roll plot 3 · Roll plot 4. NYSDOT also posts the open house boards and the rest of the project documents on its project page.

  6. Fall 2026Third public meeting expected
  7. 2028–29Construction expected to start

The work behind the plan

NYSDOT has spent the two years since, and hundreds of thousands of dollars, collecting, analyzing and modeling the corridor: turning counts at 20 intersections and a traffic model of the full stretch, a parking inventory, an ADA assessment of every curb ramp and pedestrian signal, a pavement evaluation from the New Rochelle line to the Rye town line, a ground and aerial survey, and three years of crash records.

What came back is a comprehensive Complete Streets redesign built to modern safety standards, with the design work paid for by the state.
