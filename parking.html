Parking & business

Parking matters. NYSDOT planned for it.

Shops, restaurants and neighbors along Boston Post Road depend on curb parking. NYSDOT studied how it's used and kept most of it, and where spaces go, it's usually for safety.

Parking

Curb parking keeps businesses open and gives neighbors somewhere to put their cars, and NYSDOT treated it that way. It studied how parking along the corridor is actually used before drawing the plan, and kept it where it's needed. Its April 2026 plans keep most of it, several blocks gain spaces, and many of the spaces that go are rarely used.

Where spaces are removed or moved, it's usually for safety:

  • Longer bus stops. Modern stops run about 100 feet instead of 30, so a bus can pull fully to the curb instead of stopping in a travel lane. Several stops also move across an intersection.
  • Clear sight lines at intersections, so drivers pulling out of side streets can see people crossing and cars coming.
  • Curb extensions, which shorten the distance people have to walk across the road.
  • The bike lane, in a few stretches where the road is too narrow for everything.

Where we think the plan can do better

Safe Routes to School volunteers went through NYSDOT's plans block by block. We think NYSDOT did a good job balancing parking, safety and traffic flow, and we found places where it could do better. We've passed these to the villages and town, since several are best solved locally:

  • Downtown Larchmont. Some spaces are lost to decorative buffers and generous clearance around rarely used driveways. Working with NYSDOT, the village could win back some or most of them.
  • Orienta to Mamaroneck Avenue. Fitting a bike lane on the street takes out a lane of parking used by Harbor Island and the businesses across from the water treatment plant. Moving the bike path into the park would be nicer, safer, and keep that parking.
  • Delancey and Orienta. Changing access at Delancey, perhaps with no left turns, could make the proposed roundabout unnecessary, saving money and Harbor Island Park land.

Some spaces can't come back under any design: a bus stop built to today's standards is simply longer than the one it replaces.

NYSDOT is still refining the parking layout. The latest version is in the project documents.

Local businesses

Business owners ask whether fewer lanes mean fewer customers. It's a fair question, and it has been studied more than most people realize.

The big picture · Volker & Handy, UC Davis, 2021

23 studies, 45 streets, 16 cities

Researchers at UC Davis gathered every original US and Canadian study from 2000 to 2020 that measured what happened to nearby businesses after a street was changed for walking or biking, or compared what shoppers spend by how they arrive.

spending studies found people who walk or bike spend more per month than drivers, because they come more often. Two confirmed it statistically.

The caution: three studies found that businesses built around the car, such as auto parts and repair shops, gas stations and large home-goods stores, might see flat or falling sales.

The authors' conclusion: the weight of the evidence points to a positive, or at least non-negative, effect on shops and restaurants, even where travel lanes or parking are removed.

The studies closest to Boston Post Road are below.

Four-lane roads that became three

Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines · 2010

Traffic held, crashes fell by half, most businesses saw no change

A two-mile road diet on a street of small, locally owned shops and restaurants carrying 11,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day, similar to Boston Post Road. Traffic volumes did not drop. Most business owners saw no effect, 23% reported more shopping, and a majority favored keeping it.

A case study: owners' reports, not audited sales.

York Boulevard, Los Angeles · 2006

No difference from the half of the street that kept four lanes

Half of the corridor got a road diet; the other half didn't. Property values, new business openings and business closures showed no significant difference between the halves. Parking was not removed.

One corridor, studied in a UCLA master's project.

Actual sales, compared with similar streets

Seattle, 7 safety projects · 2024

Sales grew the same as at 18 comparison sites

Taxable sales near the projects rose $1.20 million a year, against $1.14 million at matched sites with no changes. The difference is not statistically significant. The authors conclude these projects are not linked to harm for nearby businesses.

Several kinds of safety projects, not only lane reductions.

14 streets in 6 cities · 2020

Retail did better on 9 of 14 improved streets

Portland State compared improved streets with similar ones that weren't changed. Retail was better on 9, unchanged on 2 and mixed on 3. A 2008 road diet on Lyndale Avenue in Minneapolis saw retail sales rise.

Urban streets. Two corridors showed signs of harm in one sector.

9th Avenue, Manhattan · 2013

Local retail sales up 49% in three years

One of four travel lanes became a protected bike lane and turn lanes, and the parking stayed. Sales at local ground-floor shops rose 49%, against 3% across Manhattan.

Manhattan, and nearby comparison streets also grew strongly.

Bloor Street, Toronto · 2017

Merchants overestimated how many customers drive

Measured before and after a bike lane, against a similar street without one: visitors came more often and vacancies held steady. Most merchants believed at least a quarter of their customers drove; fewer than one in ten did.

A dense downtown street. Larchmont shoppers drive more.

What it means here

The review's caution about car-oriented businesses matters here: Boston Post Road has gas stations and repair shops, and their concerns about driveway access deserve direct answers from NYSDOT.

The practical lessons: keep parking and loading where businesses need it, which is the aim of the improvements we've passed along, and measure sales and parking before construction so the result can be checked afterward.
