Parking & business

Most parking stays

A road diet changes the travel lanes, not the curb. Most parking on Boston Post Road stays where it is.

Parking

Most existing parking remains. Some spaces would be removed or moved where the room is needed for safety:

  • Longer bus stops, so buses can pull fully to the curb instead of stopping in a travel lane.
  • Clear sight lines at intersections, so drivers turning out of side streets can see people crossing and cars coming.
  • Curb extensions, which shorten the distance people have to walk across the road.

NYSDOT is still refining the parking layout. The latest version is in the project documents.

Local businesses

Business owners ask whether fewer lanes mean fewer customers. It's a fair question, and it has been studied. Across the careful studies, the effect on nearby businesses is usually neutral to positive. Where businesses were hurt, parking and loading space had been taken away. Boston Post Road keeps most of its parking.

14 streets in 6 cities · 2020

Retail did better on 9 of 14 improved streets

Portland State compared streets that got bike and pedestrian improvements with similar streets that didn't. Retail was better on 9, unchanged on 2 and mixed on 3. The closest match to ours, a 2008 road diet on Lyndale Avenue in Minneapolis, saw retail sales rise.

All urban streets. Two corridors showed signs of harm in one sector.

9th Avenue, Manhattan · 2013

Local retail sales up 49% in three years

One of four travel lanes became a protected bike lane and turn lanes, and the parking stayed. Sales at local ground-floor shops rose 49%, against 3% across Manhattan.

Manhattan is not Larchmont, and nearby comparison streets also grew strongly.

37 Complete Streets projects · 2015

More businesses, higher property values

Smart Growth America looked at projects across the country, including small towns and an NYSDOT project in Hamburg, NY. Every community that tracked business counts gained businesses, and property values rose in 8 of 10.

Local agencies reported their own results, with few comparison areas.

Bloor Street, Toronto · 2017

Merchants overestimated how many customers drive

Before and after a bike lane, measured against a similar street without one: visitors came more often and vacancies held steady. Most merchants believed at least a quarter of their customers drove; fewer than one in ten did.

A dense downtown street. Larchmont shoppers drive more.

Where it went wrong

Two studies found harm, and both are worth reading. In downtown Vancouver in 2011, businesses on new separated bike lanes estimated their sales fell 4 to 10 percent; the lanes took away parking and loading zones and restricted turns. In Cambridge, Massachusetts, a 2024 federal study found no measurable difference in employment or commercial real estate, but more businesses on bike-lane streets reported falling revenue, and they pointed to lost parking.

The lesson is about the curb, not the lanes. NYSDOT's plan keeps most parking, and the spaces it moves go to bus stops, sight lines and crossings. The Cambridge study's advice is good advice here too: measure sales and parking before construction, so the result can be checked afterward.
