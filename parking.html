Parking & business

Most parking stays

A road diet changes the travel lanes, not the curb. Most parking on Boston Post Road stays where it is.

Parking

Most existing parking remains. Some spaces would be removed or moved where the room is needed for safety:

  • Longer bus stops, so buses can pull fully to the curb instead of stopping in a travel lane.
  • Clear sight lines at intersections, so drivers turning out of side streets can see people crossing and cars coming.
  • Curb extensions, which shorten the distance people have to walk across the road.

NYSDOT is still refining the parking layout. The latest version is in the project documents.

Local businesses

Business owners ask whether fewer lanes mean fewer customers. It's a fair question, and it has been studied. A 2021 review of 23 North American studies found that shops and restaurants near new walking and biking improvements generally did better or saw no significant change, whether or not travel lanes or parking were removed. The studies closest to our situation are below.

Four-lane roads that became three

Ingersoll Avenue, Des Moines · 2010

Traffic held, crashes fell by half, most businesses saw no change

A two-mile road diet on a street of small, locally owned shops and restaurants carrying 11,000 to 17,000 vehicles a day, similar to Boston Post Road. Traffic volumes did not drop. Most business owners saw no effect, 23% reported more shopping, and a majority favored keeping it.

A case study: owners' reports, not audited sales.

York Boulevard, Los Angeles · 2006

No difference from the half of the street that kept four lanes

Half of the corridor got a road diet; the other half didn't. Property values, new business openings and business closures showed no significant difference between the halves. Parking was not removed.

One corridor, studied in a UCLA master's project.

Actual sales, compared with similar streets

Seattle, 7 safety projects · 2024

Sales grew the same as at 18 comparison sites

Taxable sales near the projects rose $1.20 million a year, against $1.14 million at matched sites with no changes. The difference is not statistically significant. The authors conclude these projects are not linked to harm for nearby businesses.

Several kinds of safety projects, not only lane reductions.

14 streets in 6 cities · 2020

Retail did better on 9 of 14 improved streets

Portland State compared improved streets with similar ones that weren't changed. Retail was better on 9, unchanged on 2 and mixed on 3. A 2008 road diet on Lyndale Avenue in Minneapolis saw retail sales rise.

Urban streets. Two corridors showed signs of harm in one sector.

9th Avenue, Manhattan · 2013

Local retail sales up 49% in three years

One of four travel lanes became a protected bike lane and turn lanes, and the parking stayed. Sales at local ground-floor shops rose 49%, against 3% across Manhattan.

Manhattan, and nearby comparison streets also grew strongly.

Bloor Street, Toronto · 2017

Merchants overestimated how many customers drive

Measured before and after a bike lane, against a similar street without one: visitors came more often and vacancies held steady. Most merchants believed at least a quarter of their customers drove; fewer than one in ten did.

A dense downtown street. Larchmont shoppers drive more.

Where it went wrong

Two studies found harm, and both are worth reading. In downtown Vancouver in 2011, businesses on new separated bike lanes estimated their sales fell 4 to 10 percent; the lanes took away parking and loading zones and restricted turns. In Cambridge, Massachusetts, a 2024 federal study found no measurable difference in employment or commercial real estate, but more businesses on bike-lane streets reported falling revenue, and they pointed to lost parking.

The 2021 review adds one more caution: businesses built around the car, such as gas stations and repair shops, may not do as well as shops and restaurants. Boston Post Road has several, and their concerns about driveway access deserve direct answers from NYSDOT.

The practical lessons: keep parking and loading where businesses need it, as NYSDOT's plan largely does, and measure sales and parking before construction so the result can be checked afterward.
